How it works

Sensor measures. Arduino decides. Website shows and acts.

01 · SENSE

A waterproof sensor on the lid

The JSN-SR04T sends a sound pulse down to the water and times the echo. Shorter echo, fuller tank.

02 · DECIDE

An Arduino in a 3D-printed box

It turns the distance into a percentage, drives the screen, LEDs and buzzer, and sends a reading every second over USB or WiFi.

03 · ACT

This website and its smart agent

It charts the level, learns your daily pattern, warns before the tank runs low, flags leaks and opens a refill request.

    Lean business model

    How this links to our business model

    Parts cost about $35 per unit from our parts list. Each part of the website answers one box of our Lean Business Model Canvas.

    Problem

    People like Jad have no simple way to know how much water is left, so they lose time, energy and money when the tank runs out.

    Solution

    A sensor inside the tank, an Arduino that reads it, and this website that shows the level, warns early and helps order a refill.

    Unique value

    Low cost and easy to understand. It also refills the tank by itself when city water is available, and checks the water quality.

    Key metrics

    (1) Number of kits sold. (2) Shortages avoided, counted live by the smart agent above: 0 so far.

    Revenue streams

    Kit sale, a monthly premium plan for the smart agent and history, and a fee per refill ordered through the website.

    Next upgrade

    WiFi works at home through the ESP32 bridge. Next: an online service so Jad can check the tank from anywhere, not just on his own WiFi.

    Hardware

    Arduino guide

    Build MyWater one step at a time. Each picture shows the real Arduino UNO and breadboard; new wires are bright, earlier ones are faded (on a phone, pinch to zoom). Every + rail is 5V and every − rail is GND.

    Jump to step
    Step 1

    Power rails

    • Arduino 5V → + rail beside a (number 29). Arduino GND → − rail beside a (number 26).
    • Join the rails: + to + at number 27, − to − at number 28 (one wire each, across the board).
    • Rail holes come in groups of 5 with gaps at 6, 12, 18, 24, 30. Any hole along the same coloured line is the same.
    Step 2

    Ultrasonic sensor (JSN-SR04T)

    • 5V → + rail beside j (number 3), GND → − rail beside j (number 7).
    • Trig → pin 9 (orange), Echo → pin 10 (green).
    • Plug the probe cable into the white socket on the sensor board.
    Step 3

    Screen (LCD with I2C backpack)

    • The backpack sits on the back of the screen: its pin 1 on the screen's GND pin.
    • GND → − rail beside j (number 29), VCC → + rail beside j (number 28).
    • SDA → A4 (blue), SCL → A5 (purple).

    Step 3b Screen + backpack without soldering

    • No soldering and no female–female wires? Use a second small breadboard, only for the screen.
    • Backpack flat, chips up: its 16 pins in 8e … 23e (pin 1, next to the 4 side pins, in 8e). Screen flat, face up: its 16 pins in 8a … 23a (GND in 8a, BLK in 23a).
    • Same column = same metal strip, so every backpack pin touches the matching screen pin. Keep everything else out of columns 8–23.
    • Male–female wires on the 4 side pins: SCL → A5, SDA → A4, VCC → + rail and GND → − rail of the main breadboard (28 and 29, beside j).
    • Or, simplest of all: solder the backpack onto the screen at the Red Room (5 minutes), then follow Step 3.
    Step 4

    Three LEDs

    • Red: pin 7 → 6a, resistor 6b–9b, LED long leg 9e, short leg 10e, black jumper 10a → − rail beside a.
    • Yellow: pin 6 → 12a, resistor 12b–15b, LED 15e (long) and 16e, jumper 16a → − rail.
    • Green: pin 5 → 18a, resistor 18b–21b, LED 21e (long) and 22e, jumper 22a → − rail.
    Step 5

    Buzzer and mute button

    • Buzzer + leg in 1c, other leg in 4c. Pin 8 → 1a, black jumper 4a → − rail beside a.
    • Button legs in 24e, 26e, 24f, 26f (across the middle gap). Pin 2 → 24a, black jumper 26j → − rail beside j.
    • No resistor for the button: the code turns on the Arduino's own one.
    Step 6

    Main build: check everything

    • This is the whole basic MyWater on the breadboard.
    • Before plugging in USB: check that no + wire touches a − hole.
    • Upload the code: the 3 LEDs light in turn and the buzzer beeps once.
    Step 7

    Add-on: flow sensor and water quality (TDS)

    • Flow sensor: red → + rail beside j (2), black → − rail beside j (1), yellow → pin 3.
    • TDS board: + → + rail beside a (2), − → − rail beside a (3), A → A0.
    • The TDS probe goes in the tank; the flow sensor goes on the pipe to the house.
    Step 8

    Add-on: clock (DS3231)

    • VCC → + rail beside j (23), GND → − rail beside j (25).
    • SDA → the SDA pin next to AREF, SCL → the SCL pin next to it.
    • These two pins are the same as A4 / A5, which the screen already uses.
    Step 9

    Add-on: relay and 12V valve

    • Relay VCC → + rail beside a (25), GND → − rail beside a (23), IN → pin 11.
    • 12V adapter + → relay COM. Relay NO → valve +. Valve − → adapter −.
    • Diode across the valve, grey stripe on +. The 12V never touches the Arduino.
    Step 10

    Add-on: WiFi (ESP32)

    • A1 → 8j. 1 kΩ from 8i to 11i. Jumper 11g → 13g. 2 kΩ from 13h to the − rail beside j. (5V becomes 3.3V.)
    • 13f → ESP32 RX2 (orange). ESP32 TX2 → A2 (purple). ESP32 GND → − rail beside a (20).
    • Power the ESP32 from its own USB charger.

    How the distance becomes a percentage: level = (empty − d) ÷ (empty − full) × 100. The sound travels to the water and back at 343 m/s, so d = echo time × 0.0343 ÷ 2 cm.

    About 20 minutes once the parts are wired.

    1. Install the Arduino IDE and the screen library

      Download the IDE from arduino.cc. Then open Tools → Manage Libraries, install LiquidCrystal I2C by Frank de Brabander and RTClib by Adafruit (needed even without the clock module).

    2. Upload the sketch

      Open MyWater_Arduino.ino, choose Tools → Board → Arduino UNO and the right Port, then press Upload. The LEDs light one after the other and the buzzer beeps once: that is the self-test.

    3. Check the readings

      Open the Serial Monitor at 9600 baud. You should see one line per second like {"d":43.2,"lvl":57.4,...}. If "ok":0 appears, check the TRIG and ECHO wires. Then close the Serial Monitor: only one program can use the USB port at a time.

    4. Open this website in Chrome or Edge on a laptop

      Click Connect Arduino (USB) and pick the port that says Arduino or USB Serial. Safari and Firefox do not support USB connections from websites, and phones cannot do it either.

    5. Calibrate on the real tank

      Mount the probe on the lid at least 25 cm above the highest water level (the sensor cannot measure closer than about 20–25 cm). With the tank empty, click Use reading as empty. With it full, click Use reading as full. Enter the tank size in litres and click Save and send to Arduino. The Arduino keeps these settings after a power cut.

    6. Test the alarm

      Lower the water (or raise the alert level above the current level). The red LED, the buzzer and the banner on this page should all switch on together. Press the button on the box to mute it.

    7. Fit the add-on modules

      At the top of the code, set HAS_FLOW, HAS_TDS, HAS_RTC and HAS_VALVE to true only for the modules you fitted. Fit the flow sensor and the valve with the arrow on their body pointing the way the water flows. The valve needs some water pressure to open (at least 0.02 MPa), so put it on the city-water pipe, not under the tank. If the valve opens when it should close, set RELAY_ACTIVE_LOW to false.

    8. Optional: WiFi

      To use MyWater without a cable, add the ESP32 bridge. The steps are on the WiFi tab.

    9. Sync the clock and set the valve

      On the dashboard, click Sync clock once. Then choose the valve mode, the fill levels and, if your building gets city water only at certain hours, tick Only fill during city-water hours.

    Use MyWater on any phone or laptop on your WiFi, with no USB cable. The Arduino UNO has no WiFi, so a small ESP32 board (about $7) works as a bridge: it carries the readings to WiFi and hosts this website itself.

    1. Wire the ESP32

      Follow Step 10 on the Build steps tab: UNO A1 → 1 kΩ → ESP32 GPIO16, a 2 kΩ from GPIO16 to GND, ESP32 GPIO17 → UNO A2, and GND to GND. Power the ESP32 from its own USB phone charger.

    2. Add ESP32 support to the Arduino IDE

      File → Preferences → Additional boards manager URLs: https://espressif.github.io/arduino-esp32/package_esp32_index.json. Then Tools → Board → Boards Manager and install esp32 by Espressif Systems.

    3. Type your WiFi name and password

      Open MyWater_ESP32_WiFi.ino (keep website_gz.h in the same folder) and fill in WIFI_NAME and WIFI_PASSWORD.

    4. Upload to the ESP32

      Tools → Board → ESP32 Dev Module, pick its port, press Upload. If it hangs on "Connecting…", hold the BOOT button on the ESP32 for 2 seconds.

    5. Upload the UNO code again

      Make sure HAS_WIFI is true at the top of MyWater_Arduino.ino, then upload it to the UNO as before.

    6. Open MyWater on your phone

      Connect the phone to the same WiFi and open http://mywater.local. If that name does not work (some Android phones), use the address shown on the LCD's WiFi page, for example http://192.168.1.23. The website connects by itself.

    7. No WiFi at the place?

      The ESP32 then makes its own network called MyWater-Setup (password mywater123). Join it and open http://192.168.4.1.

    This works on the same WiFi network. Checking the tank from anywhere (for example from university) would need an online service in between; that is a possible next step.
